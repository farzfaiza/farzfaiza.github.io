Social Isolation and Grooming Behavior Across Two Generations of Fruit Flies

A four-person class study of whether a week of social isolation changes a grooming measure in fruit flies, and in their offspring. We presented it at the Symposium for Undergraduates in Neuroscience (SUN) at Georgia State University.

At a glance

What we investigated
Whether primary and intergenerational social isolation change behaviors associated with depression-like and anxiety-like states in Drosophila melanogaster.
Context
Group project in Intro to Neuroscience Research, Georgia State University. Materials came from classroom supplies.
Team and my role
Four group members. The work was shared equally, and I was one of the four.
Methods and skills
Experimental design, fly handling, dye-based grooming assay, spectrophotometry, Western blot, two-way ANOVA, scientific writing and poster presentation
Data collected
One trial. 120 flies in the parent generation (60 control, 60 isolated), then their offspring.
Verified outcome
Presented at SUN. No peer-reviewed publication.

The question

How do primary and intergenerational social isolation affect behaviors associated with depression-like and anxiety-like states in fruit flies?

Our prediction was that isolated flies would show more of these behaviors than regular flies, and that the changes would be stronger in their offspring. Our hypothesis, drawn from published work on isolation stress, was that isolation disrupts dopamine and serotonin signaling and that epigenetic changes could carry the effect to the next generation. We did not measure dopamine, serotonin or epigenetic changes, so that part of the hypothesis was not tested.

Methods

  1. Groups. 120 flies less than one week old were split into a control group of 60 and an experimental group of 60. Control flies lived in vials with fresh food under standard conditions.
  2. Isolation. Each experimental fly spent one week alone in a small tube with ventilation holes and a little food.
  3. Offspring. After isolation, a mating group of 30 flies from each condition was set up in fresh vials. The rest went to the grooming assay. The offspring were then tested the same way.
  4. Grooming measure. Flies groomed for 20 minutes in a chamber with Brilliant Yellow dye. The dye was then washed from each fly into ethanol and read in a plate reader at 397 nm. Standards of known concentration and a blank were included.
  5. Analysis. Two-way ANOVA for sex (male, female) and condition (control, isolated), run separately for the parent and offspring generations, with Fisher's LSD for follow-up comparisons. Western blot looked at calbindin-32, a calcium-binding protein.

Our plan also included randomly shaking the isolated flies. We skipped that stressor in this first experiment.

Evidence

Figures come from our group's draft write-up. The axes are not labeled in the draft figures, and each point is one fly.

What the draft reports

  • Parents. No significant interaction between sex and condition. Isolated males were slightly higher in the numbers, but not significantly.
  • Offspring. Experimental males scored significantly higher than control males. Females showed no significant difference between conditions. Among controls, females scored higher than males. Among the experimental group, there was no sex difference.
  • Calbindin-32. From the Western blot, the order from highest to lowest was female control, female isolated, male control, male isolated. This was a single blot, described by order only.

Interpretation (our reading, not the data)

These are ideas to test, not conclusions.

The offspring result for males, with nothing visible in the parents, could point to an effect of parental isolation that appears one generation later and differs by sex. Lower calbindin-32 in isolated males could fit that pattern. Our draft raised epigenetic changes and a greater sensitivity of males as possible explanations. We did not test either one.

Limitations

  • One trial, with limited sample sizes in every group.
  • Fisher's LSD does not correct for multiple comparisons. We chose it to keep sensitivity to interactions, which makes false positives more likely.
  • The dye measure is an indirect stand-in for depression-like or anxiety-like behavior. It does not show a mood state.
  • Only isolation was tested. The shaking stressor was left out.
  • Dopamine, serotonin and epigenetic changes were not measured.

Outcomes

We presented the study as a poster at SUN. The write-up is a draft and has not been published or peer reviewed. The poster itself is not available to me, so this page is built from the project plan and our draft write-up.

Possible next steps (not done)

Repeat the experiment with larger groups and more than one trial, add the shaking stressor from our original plan, and measure the signaling and epigenetic changes that the hypothesis proposed.
